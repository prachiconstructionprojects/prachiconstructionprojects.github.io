What An Airjet Shed Must Support

Airjet looms insert weft with compressed air, so loose fibre, dust and uneven support can affect running. The shed is therefore part of the machine setup. At Prachi Construction Projects, we start with the machine layout rather than a standard shed drawing, then work outward to the building envelope and services.

The first decision is what the room must do: hold the machines steady, provide clear working aisles, allow cleaning, carry service lines and give the operator a safe route around each row. Our airjet loom shed construction service covers how we build it; this guide covers what an owner should confirm before work begins.

Requirements we review together

  • Machine layout, support points and installation clearance.
  • Working height and clear movement above the looms.
  • Floor level, finish and joint treatment across rows.
  • Power, compressed air and ventilation routes.

When an owner asks what makes an airjet shed different, we point to repetition. One loom is a machine problem; a row of looms is a building problem. The floor must behave the same way at every support point, the aisle must remain clear through the whole run, and services must arrive at each position without a patchwork of surface additions. We build with that repetition in mind.

We also look at how the unit will be cleaned. Loose fibre and dust collect around machine bases and along walls, so the finish and the corner details should allow routine cleaning without damaging the floor. The cleaning method is agreed with the owner; where a special finish or chemical is proposed, the suitability is [CONFIRM WITH OWNER / STRUCTURAL ENGINEER] before it is written into the estimate.

Height And Column Spacing Are Not Guesswork

Owners often ask for a single useful height and bay size. We do not give one, because the right values depend on the loom model, the approved structural design, material handling and the way the owner uses the aisle. Clear height is [CONFIRM WITH OWNER / STRUCTURAL ENGINEER]. Column spacing and grid positions are [CONFIRM WITH OWNER / STRUCTURAL ENGINEER]. We record those notes in the estimate so nobody builds to a borrowed number.

Columns should support the structure without blocking machine rows or trolley movement. We look at the alignment from the machine layout first, then ask the designer to confirm it. If a column falls in a working aisle, we raise it before the foundation stage; changing it later is expensive and disruptive.

Ask these questions early

Will a future row need the same aisle? Is there a overhead service route that needs clear space? Does the material route pass through the same bay as the machine foundation? These questions shape the grid as much as the structural calculation does.

We also review where maintenance will happen. A column may be structurally acceptable yet awkward for a trolley, a ladder or an air line. Walking the intended route on the drawing with the owner often reveals those conflicts before a single excavation is made. Once the grid is agreed, we mark it as the current revision and keep superseded copies out of the site file.

Flooring Sets The Tone For Every Loom Row

A loom should sit on a stable, even base. We review floor levels across each row, the finish specified for the machine interface and the way joints meet the foundation blocks. Floor tolerance and finish are [CONFIRM WITH OWNER / STRUCTURAL ENGINEER]. We do not claim a universal value, because different suppliers and designs ask for different conditions.

During construction, we keep the machine blocks and the surrounding floor in one coordinated setting-out exercise. That prevents a block from standing proud of the finished floor or a service trench from crossing a row. The loom foundation service and our foundation guide describe that interface in more detail.

What we check before finishing

  • Levels at machine points and in working aisles.
  • Anchor pockets, grout areas and block edges.
  • Drain or trench positions where a wet area is involved.
  • Finish consistency where operators walk or move material.

Humidity, Dust, Power And Compressed Air

The room around an airjet loom should be managed as part of the process. Dust and loose fibre need a cleaning plan; humidity needs to stay within the range the machine supplier specifies; power and compressed air need to reach each row without ugly surface runs. The target humidity range is [CONFIRM WITH OWNER] from the machine or process supplier. Electrical load and air pressure are [CONFIRM WITH OWNER].

We plan routes in the drawing stage, not after the floor is complete. That means deciding where cables, air lines and distribution points will sit and how they will be protected from daily movement. Ventilation and enclosure choices are reviewed with the owner and the relevant specialist; we do not present them as a substitute for a process design.

If the unit includes a wet process or a processing house nearby, drainage and floor falls must be kept separate from the loom area unless the design says otherwise. See our processing house civil work for that side of the project.

A practical lesson from site coordination is that service points are easier to protect when they are part of the same sketch as the anchors. If the electrician works from one sheet and the civil crew from another, a distribution point can land in a working aisle. We bring those lines together early and confirm the final connection positions with the supplier before the floor is closed.

How We Sequence The Work

We begin with site review and setting out, then move to foundations, structure, floor, services and finishes, with checks at each stage. The sequence is adjusted to the approved design and the owner’s machine delivery plan. We do not promise a timeline without the full brief; the programme is [CONFIRM WITH OWNER] once scope and dependencies are known.

Communication matters during this stage. The owner, machine supplier, designer and site team need to know which decision is blocking the next activity. We keep those decisions visible so a missing drawing or an unconfirmed anchor does not surface on the day of installation.

A useful coordination habit

Keep one drawing set as the current set. When a revision arrives, mark the old set superseded. That small discipline prevents two trades from working from different row spacing or different service points.

Checks Before You Release The Shed

Before handover, walk each row with the machine team. Check that levels, anchors, power points, air points and aisles match the agreed layout. Confirm that cleaning and material movement will not damage the finish.
