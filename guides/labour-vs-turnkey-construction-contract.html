Labour vs Turnkey Construction Contract: Which Suits Your Textile Unit?

Choose labour-only when you want to buy and manage materials yourself; choose a labour-and-material or turnkey contract when you want one team responsible for crew, materials and site management. At Prachi Construction Projects, we put the scope, exclusions and decision points in writing before work starts, so both sides know what is being handled.

What Each Contract Actually Covers

In a labour-only arrangement, the contractor provides workers and site management while the owner buys materials. In a labour-and-material arrangement, the contractor handles both under one scope. A turnkey promise can mean different things to different firms, so we ask for a written list rather than relying on the word alone. Our services page lists the work we normally handle.

Neither arrangement is automatically better. The right one depends on how much time the owner can give to procurement, how quickly decisions must be made and where risk should sit. This guide is about choosing a structure that matches the project, not selling a label.

The three responsibilities to separate

  • Who supplies labour and site supervision.
  • Who buys, stores and replaces materials.
  • Who is responsible for coordination with the designer and machine supplier.

The words used in a proposal matter less than the responsibility table behind them. We encourage owners to make a simple list: labour, materials, supervision, design coordination, testing, cleanup and handover. Put a name against each line. The result tells you what the contract really is, whatever title appears on the cover.

When Labour-Only Makes Sense

Labour-only can suit an owner who already has reliable suppliers and wants control over material choices. It can also suit a project where certain items are supplied directly by the machine vendor. The owner must be able to keep material availability in step with the site programme, or crews will wait.

We have found that this arrangement works best when responsibilities are split cleanly. The contractor needs to know who will deliver steel, cement, flooring material and special items, and what happens if a delivery is short. Those details belong in the written scope. Material rates and supply responsibilities are [CONFIRM WITH OWNER] in the agreement.

The owner role in labour-only

The owner becomes an active procurement manager. If that is not realistic, the apparent saving can be lost in delay and rework. Be honest about available time before choosing this route.

Labour-only also requires storage discipline. Materials arriving from different suppliers must be checked and stacked so the crew can use them in sequence. If a critical item is delayed, the site team may have to change the work order, which can affect finished areas. The owner accepts that coordination role in this arrangement.

When Labour And Material Suits The Project

A labour-and-material contract suits owners who want one point of responsibility for the civil package. The contractor arranges crew and materials and manages the sequence. This does not remove owner decisions: approvals, design changes and machine interfaces still need clear instructions.

For textile units, the advantage is coordination. Foundations, floor, drains and service routes are handled by one team reading the same drawing. Our foundation guide and cost factors guide show why those interfaces need attention.

The contract should still list the material basis and any exclusions. If a particular finish or brand is important, write it in. If it is open, say who approves a substitute before it is used.

A single contract does not remove the need for owner decisions. The owner still approves drawings, finishes, machine interfaces and any change in scope. What changes is the number of parties the owner must chase. For a busy unit owner, that reduction in coordination can be more valuable than a headline saving.

Write The Scope So There Is No Guesswork

A good scope names the drawing revision, the work included, the work excluded and the assumptions used for pricing. It should state who handles site development, foundations, structure, floors, drains, finishes and cleanup. Where a number is needed for an allowance or payment term, record it as [CONFIRM WITH OWNER] until the owner and contractor agree.

The scope should also describe how coordination will happen. Who attends a machine interface meeting? Who signs off a setting-out drawing? Who confirms a floor finish? These questions are not bureaucracy; they prevent a crew from waiting for an answer.

Items often left unclear

  • Material substitutions and approval responsibility.
  • Testing and documentation for materials.
  • Which services stop at the building interface.
  • Who restores an area after another trade cuts into it.

Handover standards should also be written down. State what will be cleaned, which snag points will be closed and what records will be given to the owner. A contractor and an owner who agree on the finish condition at the start rarely argue about it at the end.

Handle Changes With A Record

Changes are normal in construction. A machine layout may be revised, a drain route may move, or an owner may decide to add a future-ready service point. What matters is that the change is recorded before it is built. We keep a simple written note of the instruction and its effect on scope.

Payment terms should follow the same clarity. The milestone structure and any advance or retention arrangement are [CONFIRM WITH OWNER] and belong in the signed agreement. A handshake understanding is not enough for a substantial civil project.

We also recommend one person on each side who can approve a change. If several people can instruct the crew, the site team may receive conflicting directions. A named approver slows nothing down; it simply makes the instruction traceable.

A Simple Decision Checklist

Ask yourself whether you can source materials quickly, whether you want to approve every brand, and whether you have time to manage deliveries. If the answer is yes, labour-only may fit. If you want a single team to coordinate civil work around machine layouts, a labour-and-material contract may be simpler.

Whichever route you choose, use the same checklist: current drawings, written scope, clear exclusions, named approvals and a change record. For discussion, call +91 76004 90851 or visit our contact page. You can also read our contractor checklist before signing.

Think about the owner’s working life after handover too. If the unit is running while later phases are built, the contract should state how access, cleaning and safety boundaries will be handled. A clause that looks minor on paper can decide whether production and construction can coexist calmly.
