Waterjet Loom Shed Requirements: Water, Drainage and Floor Planning

A waterjet loom shed is planned around water: supply to each row, controlled floor falls, drains that do not cross dry routes, and a civil interface with the ETP that your consultant approves. At Prachi Construction Projects, we settle these lines on the drawing before the floor is cast, because water mistakes are hard to correct after machines arrive.

Plan The Water Before The Floor

A waterjet loom uses water in the weaving process. That changes the building more than any general finish schedule. Water has to arrive where the machine needs it, leave the point of use quickly and travel somewhere that does not damage the floor or cross a dry working route. We start every waterjet discussion with this simple circuit.

Our waterjet loom shed construction service describes the civil work. This guide looks at the owner decisions that must be made before that work starts. The supply point, collection point and floor level are [CONFIRM WITH OWNER / STRUCTURAL ENGINEER] until the layout and consultant inputs are final.

The three lines to draw first

  • Fresh water route to each machine row.
  • Used water collection at the machine and along the floor.
  • Safe dry route for operators, material and finished goods.

Owners sometimes describe the water arrangement as a small plumbing item. We treat it as a civil decision because every channel, sleeve and fall has to be built into the floor. Once the slab is in place, moving a collection point means breaking finished work and risking the machine installation programme. That is why we ask for the water sketch before we finalise the floor drawing.

We also ask what happens during a stoppage. If collection cannot handle the water when a machine is paused or serviced, the nearest dry route is the first place water will go. The owner and supplier should agree how that situation is managed. The civil drawing can then show a route that is practical rather than merely neat on paper.

Supply Lines, Channels And Floor Falls

Supply lines and collection channels should be visible on the same drawing as the machine layout. We look for straight, maintainable routes and avoid placing a channel where a trolley or operator will constantly cross it. Where the floor needs a fall toward a drain, the direction and value come from the approved design; the floor slope is [CONFIRM WITH OWNER / STRUCTURAL ENGINEER].

During site work, we check channels and embeds before the finish goes down. A drain that is slightly out of line can leave water sitting near a machine block, and a covered channel that cannot be opened becomes a maintenance problem. We prefer simple details that the owner can understand and maintain.

If the unit also has dyeing or washing areas, those flows should be reviewed with the process designer, not merged into the loom shed by habit. Our processing house civil work page covers the separate wet-area work.

Maintainability shapes our details. A channel that can be opened and cleaned is more useful than one that disappears under a finish nobody wants to disturb. We discuss access covers, corners and junctions with the owner, then coordinate them with the floor programme. Cover details and channel sizes are [CONFIRM WITH OWNER / STRUCTURAL ENGINEER] until the designer and supplier agree.

Where several rows share a collection line, we check that the route does not force an awkward turn at a machine base. A small change in the drawing can make the daily work much easier. We prefer to spend that time at the drawing table rather than asking a crew to improvise around a finished block.

Keep Wet And Dry Routes Separate

The practical problem in many weaving units is not the presence of water but where it travels. We try to keep the wet strip close to the machine and the dry walking and storage routes away from it. That means setting out the aisle, the drain line and the material stack position together.

Where space is limited, the owner must decide which movement matters most. We can suggest a workable civil layout, but the final process flow belongs to the owner and machine supplier. We record any unresolved point rather than forcing a route that will be changed later.

Questions we ask about separation

Where will finished fabric be placed? Does cleaning water flow toward that area? Can a maintenance worker open a channel without moving a machine? The answers often change the layout more than a fresh coat of finish ever will.

Storage is part of this conversation. Finished fabric and cartons should not sit where cleaning water can reach them, and operators should not have to step across a wet channel to reach an exit. We mark those routes on the layout and ask the owner to confirm them. If the plot is constrained, we explain the trade-off rather than pretending every requirement can be met.

Connect Civil Work To The ETP Interface

The civil contractor needs to know where process water leaves the building, but the treatment system is a specialist scope. We coordinate the drain outfall and the physical opening with the owner’s consultant and the ETP supplier. Any connection size, level or treatment requirement is [CONFIRM WITH OWNER] from those responsible parties.

We do not state a rule about what must be treated or how it must be treated. That depends on the process, local requirements and the approved design. Owners should verify the position with the relevant authorities and a qualified consultant before construction, and should make sure the same information reaches the civil designer.

A useful practical step is to fix the interface point on the drawing: where the building drain ends and the ETP work begins. That avoids disputes later about who provides the last section of pipe, the chamber and the floor reinstatement.

The handover point between building drains and treatment work should be visible to everyone. We like a drawing note that says where the civil scope ends and what is provided beyond that point. It avoids a common site argument about the last chamber, the last pipe and who restores the floor after a late connection.

Foundations And Finishes That Survive Daily Washing

Machine blocks in a waterjet shed must work with the floor around them. We set out the blocks from supplier information and keep the surrounding finish consistent with the agreed drain falls. Block dimensions, anchor details and floor tolerance are [CONFIRM WITH OWNER / STRUCTURAL ENGINEER]. The loom foundation service explains how we build these points.

The finish should be chosen for the actual cleaning method, not just for how it looks on handover day. We discuss this with the owner and supplier and write the agreed basis into the estimate. If a product is proposed later, we check it against the approved design before substituting it.

Where water usually exposes weak detailing

  • A channel without access for cleaning.
  • A floor fall that points toward a dry storage route.
  • A machine block edge that cannot be sealed properly.
  • An outfall position that was never fixed on the drawing.

Handover Checks With The Machine Team

At handover, walk the shed with the machine team and run water through the agreed route. Check that collection works, that no dry route is flooded and that access to channels remains possible. Record any snag points and close them before installation. The programme for these checks is [CONFIRM WITH OWNER].

We also keep a simple record of any temporary connection used during testing. Temporary arrangements are easy to forget once machines are installed. Removing them, sealing penetrations and closing the final snag list are part of a handover that the owner can use with confidence.
