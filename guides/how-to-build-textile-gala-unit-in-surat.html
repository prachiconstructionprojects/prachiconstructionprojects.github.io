At Prachi Construction Projects, we treat the gala as a production tool, not just a shell.

Start With The Production Brief

A gala unit is not a generic building with looms placed inside it. Before we draw a line, we ask what will be made, which machines will be installed, how material will move and what the owner expects to add later. The answers decide where columns can stand, where aisles must stay clear and which utilities need a reserved route. Our gala construction service explains the building work; this guide explains the order in which an owner should approach it.

What we ask at the first meeting

We ask for a plot sketch, a machine layout, the intended product flow and a note on wet or dry processes. If the machine supplier has installation drawings, we ask for those too. Where a value depends on design or equipment, we do not guess; we mark [CONFIRM WITH OWNER / STRUCTURAL ENGINEER] and take the answer from the qualified people responsible for it.

  • Which machines are fixed now and which may be added later.
  • Where raw material, waste and finished goods will be stored.
  • How vehicles will enter, turn and leave the plot.
  • Which services must reach each row without cutting finished floors.

This brief becomes the reference for every later decision. When a change is requested after foundations are cast, we can point back to the agreed brief and explain what has to be revisited instead of quietly absorbing the change in the field.

Check The Land Before You Commit To A Layout

We have seen good layouts fail because nobody walked the plot carefully. Boundaries, neighbour walls, approach roads, existing levels and low spots decide how easily material can be brought in and how water will leave the site. In Surat and nearby industrial belts, two neighbouring plots can behave very differently, so we treat every site visit as fresh work.

During the visit we look for space to stack materials, room for a mixer or cutting area, and a route that keeps loaded vehicles away from fresh work. We also discuss drainage and whether the plot needs filling or regrading. Ground capacity is not something we assume; we ask for a soil report and note [CONFIRM WITH OWNER / STRUCTURAL ENGINEER] wherever a bearing value or treatment is required.

How the site visit changes the plan

A tight approach may change the storage plan. A low corner may change the floor level and drain direction. A boundary close to a proposed wall may change the construction sequence. These are practical site decisions, not reasons to abandon the project; they simply need to be made before concrete is ordered.

Turn The Brief Into A Buildable Layout

The grid should follow the machine rows and the working aisles. We review column positions with the owner and designer, then coordinate clear height, roof shape, ventilation and light. Any figure for height, bay spacing, floor loading or tolerance is confirmed with the designer and machine supplier; we record it as [CONFIRM WITH OWNER / STRUCTURAL ENGINEER] rather than presenting a borrowed standard as fact.

Service routes deserve the same attention as columns. Power, compressed air, water and drains should be drawn before the slab is cast, so the finished floor is not opened later. For units that may grow, we leave the first phase coherent and flag where a future row could connect without breaking the original workflow.

Lines we coordinate together

  • Machine anchor points and surrounding floor finish.
  • Column grid, aisle widths and vehicle movement.
  • Water, drain and power routes below or across the floor.

The result should be a drawing an owner can read. If the drawing only shows a rectangle and a few columns, the building has not yet been planned for production.

Get A Written Estimate That Matches The Scope

Once the brief and drawings are clear, we prepare a written estimate that states exactly what is included. That usually means separating site work, structure, flooring, drains, finishes and any machine-foundation interface. Materials are listed by the agreed basis, and anything outside the scope is written down rather than left to assumption. We do not publish unverified rates; the applicable rate or amount is [CONFIRM WITH OWNER].

Owners often receive estimates that look similar but describe different work. One may include a particular floor build-up while another excludes it. One may assume material supplied by the owner while another includes procurement. Our cost factors page explains the drivers, and our cost factors guide goes through them in more detail.

Questions to ask before accepting a price

Ask who supplies each material, who handles testing, what drawings the price is based on and how a change will be recorded. Ask for the assumptions in writing. A lower number that leaves out drainage or floor preparation is not a saving; it is a future variation.

Build In A Sequence That Protects The Floor And Foundations

Our site sequence usually starts with setting out and site development, followed by foundations, columns, roof or slab work, walls, drains and finishes. The exact order depends on the approved design and the plot. Before each concrete stage, we check levels, positions and embedments with the people concerned. Where the supplier must approve an anchor or level, we stop and obtain that confirmation.

Loom areas need special care because the floor is working surface, not just a finish. We keep service trenches, anchor pockets and machine blocks coordinated with the slab programme. Our foundation service describes how blocks are set out; the foundation guide explains the owner checks behind that work.

Hold points worth keeping

  • Plot levels and setting out before excavation.
  • Foundation and anchor positions before concrete.
  • Drain falls and service routes before floor finish.
  • Final levels and finishes before machine installation.

Reach Production Start Without Last-Minute Rework

A building is ready for production only when the civil work and the machine interface agree. Before handover we walk the unit with the owner, list any snag points and close them. We check that access routes are usable, drains are clear, floor finishes are consistent and no service point has been buried in the wrong place.

If machines will be installed in phases, we agree which parts of the gala are handed over first and how later work will be protected. Expansion is easier when the original layout already considered it. For larger shells or storage blocks, see our industrial shed service. To discuss a specific plot, use our contact page or call +91 76004 90851.

The aim is simple: when the machine team arrives, the floor, power, water and access should be ready for them. That is what a gala unit built from a production brief should deliver.
